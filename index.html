Professional life

I am a fifth-year PhD candidate in the Applied Mathematics track at The Ohio State University, where I work in Professor Adriana Dawes' lab group. Our group studies how biochemical pathways, mechanical structures, and cell geometry interact and regulate one another to produce the complex patterns we associate with biological systems.

Foremost, I would like to be able to help people in my career, and I think I can make a difference researching mathematical biology. It is becoming increasingly important in understanding human health and medicine.

I am most interested in how networks in biology create flexibility and robustness. Biochemical networks, cell-signaling networks, and neural networks all share a puzzling property: they keep working across enormous variation in their parts and their surroundings, yet they stay adaptable enough to build very different outcomes from the same components. My work uses dynamical systems, bifurcation theory, and network analysis to ask which features of a biological system are forced by the structure of its network alone, before any rate constants or concentrations are known.

A puzzle I keep coming back to

A microscopic worm and a human are built by largely the same signaling pathways. The genes that pattern an animal's body are conserved across the animal kingdom, yet the bodies they produce could hardly be more different, and each one develops reliably in a world that never holds still. How can the same machinery be this robust and this flexible at the same time?

Our hypothesis, and the evidence so far points this way, is that the wiring of a biological network forces many of its important qualitative behaviors, while leaving the exact numbers free to drift. The network decides what kinds of outcomes are possible. Evolution and environment then tune the details.
