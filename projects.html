New in 2026SymmetryImage analysis

A high-throughput tool for quantifying symmetry in image datasets

Biological symmetry is usually reduced to a discrete, subjective label, even though real organisms are symmetric by degrees. With Punit Gandhi, Maria-Veronica Ciocanel, and Adriana Dawes, I built a user-friendly computational pipeline that measures symmetry as a continuous variable using transformation information. We prove the measure is invariant, equivariant, and Lipschitz continuous under translation, rotation, reflection, and rescaling, which means it can be applied across heterogeneous image datasets without manual landmarking or alignment.

Published in the Bulletin of Mathematical Biology. Read the paper (free access) →   Code →

Pattern formationBifurcation theory

Structural causes of pattern formation

Pattern formation is a hallmark of developmental biology, where cells within a tissue or organism differentiate in a precise spatial arrangement to form complex patterns. In recent work, we show that patterns of cell fates depend on both the cell-communication network and the chemical signals within each cell. We hope to generalize this work to pinpoint molecular causes of pattern failure.

SymmetryTuring patterns

Constrained changes in approximate symmetries of a Turing pattern

Symmetry is ubiquitous in biology and is an important predictor of survivability, fecundity, and evolvability. Although biological symmetry is often intuitive, it doesn't fit the idealized definitions of symmetry used in mathematics. Transformation Information, introduced by Gandhi et al. (2021), is a useful tool for measuring approximate symmetries in biology. I tracked approximate symmetries of a Turing PDE over time and found that the ways symmetries can change are constrained.

More detail is in my SMB 2023 poster. Related work by my advisor, Dr. Adriana T. Dawes, and collaborators: Characterizing symmetry transitions in systems with dynamic morphology.

Machine learningHematopoiesis

Modeling hematopoiesis with machine learning

My research at UC Irvine in Dr. Qing Nie's lab group worked toward using machine learning to model cell fate. In particular, I wanted to discover whether a graph neural ODE could mimic a stochastic model of hematopoiesis. The code covers both creating toy datasets from a hematopoiesis model and fitting them with neural ODEs and graph neural ODEs.

Expository writing

Two papers I wrote as an undergraduate. I am no expert on either topic, but I find both fascinating.

Intro to fractals, Barnsley's Collage Theorem, and image compression

Assuming only undergraduate real analysis, this paper builds the theory required to understand Barnsley's Collage Theorem, which lets us approximate extremely complex shapes with only a few functions. It closes with a brief discussion of how the theorem can be used for image compression.

Read the paper (PDF)

A history of sphere packing

An accessible account of the search for optimal sphere packings in n dimensions. I give the proof of the optimal packing in two dimensions, then discuss the intuition behind Thomas Hales's proof in three dimensions and Maryna Viazovska's results in dimensions 8 and 24.

Read the paper (PDF)
